## Read this first: screen sources for who wrote them

The most useful finding of this pass is a caution, not a dimension.

**A large share of publicly posted "department" oral board material is authored by a
commercial testing vendor and merely hosted by the department.** The City of Tulsa's
candidate orientation guide — a `.gov`-adjacent city URL, department letterhead, fire
department seal — carries the line *"Copyright © 2003, I/O Solutions, Inc."* on every page.
It is a vendor's document wearing a department's badge.

This is directly load-bearing for us. `CLAUDE.md` prohibits deriving Recruit content from
commercial hiring-test batteries — "National Testing Network / FireTEAM, IPMA-HR, CPS HR
**and equivalents**." I/O Solutions is an equivalent. So is whoever authored Columbus's FOAM
and Duncanville's FACT.

**Therefore: being on a city website is not sufficient screening.** Before a source informs a
rubric, check the footer, the copyright line, and whether a named instrument is being
described. Then apply the existing rule:

- **Generic job competencies** that recur across many independent sources — teamwork,
  integrity, composure — are not anybody's intellectual property. Safe.
- **A specific instrument's structure** — its sections, its scoring mechanics, its item
  format, its particular set and phrasing of dimensions — is the vendor's product. Off
  limits, even when a city has published it.

The convergent-core method below is the safe path *because* it only keeps what several
independent sources share. Anything appearing in exactly one vendor-authored document is
precisely what we must not track.

## Three findings that bear on Recruit's scope

**1. Entry-level oral boards do not test fire knowledge.** Tulsa states it outright: at no
point is a candidate assessed on knowledge of department procedures, firefighting tactics,
or any other specific job knowledge. Nothing in the other sources contradicts it, and the
whole shape of SLC's questions supports it.

This is the most useful single fact for rubric design. It means the board is testing
*attributes*, not preparation-as-study — which is why a candidate with no fire background
can still score well, and why one with a fire background can still fail. It also validates
the scope decision to ship no department-specific content: there is nothing department-
specific to teach here.

**2. The behavioural (STAR) frame is the dominant format.** UFA calls the board
"behaviourally-based." SLC teaches STAR explicitly — situation, task, action, result — and
six of its eight questions are past-behaviour prompts rather than hypotheticals. Our rubric
should almost certainly assess whether an answer is a *specific, concrete past incident*
with a stated action and outcome, versus a general statement of how the candidate believes
they behave. That is a criterion a model can apply reliably, and it is one of the most
common real failures.

**3. The written gate is vendor territory, and that confirms the scope call.** Seattle
requires FireTEAM and PSSA1 through National Testing Network before a candidate reaches the
board at all, with only the top 1,500 advancing. Columbus uses FOAM; Duncanville, FACT. The
written stage is thoroughly occupied by commercial instruments — which is exactly why
`CLAUDE.md` puts written-exam practice last, if at all. This pass found nothing to revisit
that with.

## Process facts worth reflecting in the practice product

From Tulsa (vendor-authored, so treat as one department's arrangement rather than a
universal, but the shape is common):

- Candidates get the question **in writing**, may keep it in front of them, and may take
  notes before answering.
- Answers are **time-boxed** — five minutes per question at Tulsa.
- The panel discusses and rates **after the candidate leaves the room**.
- Multi-part questions are common, and *answering every part* is called out as a failure
  point.

If our practice loop lets a candidate see the question, take a moment, and answer without a
clock, we are training them for an easier room than the real one. Worth a deliberate
decision rather than a default — flagged for you, not decided here.

## Candidate traps the sources name explicitly

Useful raw material for the "common traps" pillar. All of these are stated as advice or
warnings in the sources, not inferred:

- **Answering a question other than the one asked** — Tulsa calls this out as common, and
  attributes it to stress causing misreading rather than to ignorance.
- **Not answering every part of a multi-part question.**
- **Rambling, and repeating the same answer in different words** to fill time.
- **Poor delivery undercutting good content** — though Tulsa notes these are usually scored
  separately.
- **Being unprepared for "why this department"** — SLC's first question, and the one most
  answerable in advance.
- **Not having concrete stories ready.** SLC's own preparation advice is to spend the most
  time not rehearsing answers but reflecting on your own history until you know it well
  enough to apply it to any question.

## A proposed starting dimension set — for you to correct

**This is a proposal from the convergence above, not a recommendation from experience. It
is the part of this document that most needs your judgment, and it should not survive
contact with a fire captain unchanged.**

For the *first* rubric (one question type, per step 1), something like:

1. **Answers the question actually asked** — including every part of a multi-part question.
2. **Concrete and specific** — a real incident with a stated action and outcome, not a
   general claim about how they behave.
3. **Shows they understand the job** — what the work actually is, beyond the imagery.
4. **Attributes come through** — teamwork, composure, integrity, coachability, evidenced by
   the story rather than asserted about themselves.
5. **Structure and economy** — organised, complete, does not ramble or repeat.

Held separately, per Tulsa's practice: **delivery/communication**, rated apart from content
so a nervous candidate with a good answer is not penalised twice.

Open questions this raises for you, which no amount of reading will settle:

- Is "shows they understand the job" one criterion or the whole ballgame?
- Do we assess *what* a candidate did in their story, or only how well they told it? The
  sources are silent, and it is the difference between coaching interview skill and
  coaching character.
- Five criteria plus delivery — too many to give useful feedback on in one critique, or
  about right?

## Gaps in this pass

- **Three sources unverified** (Columbus, Duncanville, MTAS) — two 403s and one search-only.
  Worth retrying from a browser if you want them.
- **Only four departments read.** Convergence across four is suggestive, not conclusive.
  A dozen would make the common core genuinely defensible.
- **No source describes an actual scoring scale or anchor statements** — what separates a 3
  from a 4 on a given dimension. That is the part departments keep internal, and it is
  exactly the part a rubric needs most. **This is the biggest gap, and it is the one your
  own experience has to fill.**
- **Nothing here is about the readiness gap analysis** (pillar 2) — no sources on what makes
  a candidate competitive on paper. That is a separate reading pass.

**Reminder for whatever comes out of this:** rubric criteria and anchors get written in
original words. This file is internal calibration notes, not a content source to copy from.
