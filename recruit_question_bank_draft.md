## How to review this, cheaply

The economics only work if the family carries the judgment and the variants are a skim.
So review in two passes, and **do not** read 288 items in order.

**Pass 1 — families only (the real work, ~36 decisions).** For each family read the name,
the *listening for* line, and the *axis* line. Decide three things:

1. **Is this actually asked?** The evidence says yes for all 36. You have sat the panels;
   if a family reads as something no board asks, kill it and the eight items die with it.
2. **Is *listening for* right?** This is the load-bearing line — it is what a rubric will
   eventually score against, and it is the part I am least qualified to have written.
3. **Does it load the criterion I have assigned it?** Reassignments here are cheap now and
   expensive after the rubrics are written against them.

**Pass 2 — variants (a skim).** Within a surviving family, the only questions are: does
this one ask something genuinely different from its siblings, and would a panel actually
phrase it this way? Strike the duplicates and the ones that sound like a written test.
Expect to strike some — I wrote eight per family because eight was the target, not because
every family naturally has eight distinct things to ask. **Families where I think fewer
than eight survive are flagged `THIN`.** **Pass 2 is complete 2026-09-10** — see the
pass-2 record below.

**What I could not judge and you can.** Whether a variant sounds like a real captain
talking or like a course author writing. I have no ear for that and it is the single most
likely defect in this file.

## Authoring rules applied

- **Original words.** The two clean primary sources total 17 questions between them; nothing
  is lifted from either, nor from the trade-press compilation, nor from the competitor.
- **No fire knowledge required.** Every source agrees entry-level boards do not test tactics
  or procedure. An item a candidate could not answer without fire training is a defect —
  flag any you find.
- **No department specifics.** Items say "this department," never a named one.
- **Behavioral where the family is behavioral.** Past-incident prompts are phrased to elicit
  a specific incident, because that is what the rubrics discriminate on.
- **Varying retrieval demand** (§4, inventory-and-retrieval). Within a family some variants
  map easily onto a common story and some are deliberately awkward, so the bank exercises
  retrieval rather than recall of a prepared answer.
- **No item supplies its own answer** — no leading phrasing, no hint of what a good answer
  contains. That constraint is on critique; it applies here too.

# MOT — Motivation & fit

*Criterion **c2**, SME-approved. Family pass 1 + variant pass 2 complete 2026-09-10.
Nothing publishes until Grant opens publish. The most answerable-in-advance group
there is, which is why it is the highest-yield coaching target — and why a weak
answer here is unforgiving.*

## MOT-1 — Why the fire service at all

**Listening for:** a decision with a traceable origin; motive pointing at the work and the
people served rather than the imagery, the schedule, or the pension; specifics instead of
slogans.
**Axis:** what the question forces him to locate — origin, the alternative he rejected,
durability over time, the unglamorous case.
**Sources:** CJ, SLC, FH, TP.

> **Pass 1 (Grant, 2026-09-10):** KEEP as drafted after a skipped widget; recommendation
> stood.
> **Pass 2 (Grant, 2026-09-10):** KEEP all 8.

1. Why do you want to be a firefighter?
2. Take us back to the point where this stopped being an idea and became something you were actually going after. What happened?
3. What else did you seriously consider doing, and why aren't you doing it?
4. A lot of people want this job. What is it about the work itself that you want?
5. If you never got hired anywhere, what would you do instead, and what would you miss?
6. What do you think you'll be doing on the majority of your calls, and does that appeal to you?
7. Who or what is most responsible for you sitting in this chair today?
8. Has what you want out of this changed since you started going after it?

## MOT-2 — Why this department

**Listening for:** whether he has done anything to learn about the department beyond reading
its website; whether "why here" has a real answer or is interchangeable with any department.
**Axis:** depth of contact — public information, effort expended, people spoken to, forced
comparison against an alternative.
**Sources:** CJ, SLC, TP.

1. Why this department?
2. What do you know about us that you couldn't have learned from our website?
3. What have you done to find out what it's actually like to work here?
4. If you got an offer from us and from the department in the next city on the same day, how would you decide?
5. What do you think this department does differently from the next one you'd apply to?
6. Who have you talked to who works here, and what did they tell you?
7. What about this city, and the people who live in it, makes you want to work in it?
8. Say we hire you. Twenty years from now, why are you still here?

## MOT-3 — What he has done to prepare

**Listening for:** motion, and recency. Per c2 note 4 the anchors score direction and rate —
what he has done, when he last did it, what comes next. Per c2 note 1, effort is weighed
against opportunity, not against a checklist.
**Axis:** timeframe, whether the effort was required or self-directed, cost paid, forward plan.
**Sources:** SLC, TP.

1. What have you done to prepare for a career in the fire service?
2. Walk us through the last twelve months. What did you do to get ready for this?
3. What have you done to prepare that nobody required you to do?
4. What's the most recent thing you did to get ready, and when was it?
5. Of everything you've done to prepare, what changed how you think about the job?
6. What did you set out to do that you haven't finished, and what's your plan for it?
7. What has preparing for this cost you — time, money, something you gave up?
8. If we don't hire you this cycle, what will you have done differently by the next one?

## MOT-4 — What he understands the job to be

**Listening for:** realism. Whether the picture in his head is the work or the recruitment
poster, and whether he has grasped the medical share of the call volume.
**Axis:** the angle of approach — duties, hours, invisible parts, what surprised him.
**Sources:** SLC, TP.

1. What do you understand a firefighter's job to be?
2. What do you think takes up most of a firefighter's working hours?
3. What part of this job do you think the public doesn't see?
4. What have you learned about this work that surprised you?
5. Describe the job the way you'd describe it to someone who has only seen it on television.
6. What's the hardest part of this job that has nothing to do with fire?
7. How much of this job do you think is medical, and how do you feel about that?
8. What would you say to someone who told you they wanted this job because it looks exciting?

## MOT-5 — When and how he decided

**Listening for:** whether the decision has a real history behind it or was assembled for the
interview; and whether it survived contact with cost or doubt.
**Axis:** moment vs. accretion, who else was involved, the first real expenditure, doubt.
**Sources:** FH, TP.

1. When did you decide you wanted to do this?
2. Was there a single moment, or did it build? Tell us about it.
3. How old were you, and what were you doing at the time?
4. What did the people closest to you say when you told them?
5. What made you sure enough to start spending your own money on it?
6. Has there been a point since then where you doubted it? What happened?
7. What were you doing before this, and why did you leave it — or why haven't you yet?
8. What did you know about the job when you decided, and what do you know now?

# TEA — Teamwork & working relationships

*Criterion **c3**, Grant-approved / publishable 2026-09-10. Family pass 1 + variant
pass 2 complete 2026-09-10. Nothing publishes until Grant opens publish.*

## TEA-1 — Worked as part of a team toward a shared goal

**Listening for:** a specific incident he was personally in, with his own action named — not
a general claim about valuing teamwork. Per c3, whether other people appear as agents or as
scenery.
**Axis:** his role in the team, whether it went well, and whether he was the newest or the
most senior person in the room.
**Sources:** SLC, TP.

1. Tell us about a time you were part of a team that had to get something done.
2. Tell us about a time a team succeeded because of something you specifically did.
3. Tell us about a team you were on where you weren't in charge and didn't want to be.
4. Describe a time a group you were part of had to change plan partway through.
5. Tell us about the best team you've ever been part of. What made it work?
6. Tell us about a time you had to rely on someone else finishing their part before you could do yours.
7. Describe a time you were the newest person on a team. How did you fit in?
8. Tell us about a time your team failed. What was your part in it?

## TEA-2 — A teammate not carrying their share

**Listening for:** whether he addressed the person or routed around them; whether the account
is about fixing the team or about his own performance. This is the family c3's level 2 anchor
was written to catch.
**Axis:** who the person was, whether he acted or stayed silent, and whether the roles are
reversed onto him.
**Sources:** SLC, TP. *This family supplies the existing c3 review-set question.*

1. Tell us about a time you worked with someone who wasn't doing their share.
2. Tell us about a time someone you worked with was struggling and it was affecting everyone else.
3. Describe a time you had to say something uncomfortable to someone you worked alongside every day.
4. Tell us about a time you covered for someone. How long did it go on?
5. Describe a time someone wasn't pulling their weight and you decided not to say anything. How did that turn out?
6. Tell us about a time you were the one not keeping up. What did the people around you do?
7. Describe a time you took a problem about a coworker to a supervisor. What made you take it there?
8. Tell us about a time someone's poor work put other people at risk.

## TEA-3 — Working closely with people unlike him

**Listening for:** whether difference is described as something navigated or something
tolerated; whether he can name what he actually did. The trap is the answer that asserts
open-mindedness with no incident under it.
**Axis:** who was the outsider, whether the difference was demographic or temperamental, and
whether he was ever wrong about someone.
**Sources:** SLC, TP.

1. Tell us about a time you worked closely with someone very different from you.
2. Describe a time you were the one who was different from everyone else in the group.
3. Tell us about a time someone's background gave them a way of solving a problem you wouldn't have thought of.
4. Describe a time you had to work with someone whose values you didn't share.
5. This job puts you in a house with the same people for twenty-four hours at a stretch. What kind of person would you find hardest to live with, and how would you handle it?
6. Tell us about a time you got something wrong about someone.
7. Describe a time you had to earn the trust of a group that had reason not to trust you.
8. Tell us about a time you helped someone new feel part of a group.

## TEA-4 — The person he finds hardest to work with

**Listening for:** self-awareness under a question that invites complaint. The trap is a long
account of other people's faults with nothing about his own contribution.
**Axis:** direction of the mirror — them, him, a reversal, a third party's view of him.
**Sources:** TP, FH.

1. What kind of person do you find hardest to work with?
2. Describe the worst working relationship you've had. What made it that way?
3. What do you do that other people find difficult to work with?
4. Tell us about someone you didn't like but worked well with anyway. How did you manage it?
5. What would a coworker who doesn't much like you say about you?
6. Describe a time your first impression of a coworker turned out to be wrong.
7. What's your reaction when someone is doing the job differently than you would do it?
8. Tell us about a time you had to keep working with someone after a disagreement that never really got resolved.

# INT — Integrity & ethics

*Criteria **c4 / c5**, Grant-approved / publishable 2026-09-10. Family pass 1 +
variant pass 2 complete 2026-09-10. Nothing publishes until Grant opens publish.
This is the group candidates most often walk into a trap on, so it is also the
richest material for pillar 3.*

## INT-1 — A mistake, and what changed afterward

**Listening for:** whether he names a real mistake with a real cost, and whether anything
actually changed. The trap is the humblebrag mistake — "I care too much."
**Axis:** who bore the cost, whether he was caught, and whether he repeated it.
**Sources:** SLC, TP.

1. Tell us about a mistake you've made and what you learned from it.
2. Tell us about a mistake that cost someone else something.
3. Describe a time you broke a rule. What was the rule, and why did you break it?
4. Tell us about a time you got caught doing something wrong.
5. Describe a mistake nobody ever found out about. What did you do about it?
6. Tell us about a time you got feedback you didn't want to hear. What did you do with it?
7. What's the most expensive mistake you've made — in money, time, or trust?
8. Tell us about a mistake you made twice.

## INT-2 — Seeing a crew member do something wrong

**Listening for:** whether he acts, and whether he goes to the person before he goes over
their head. The trap at entry level is the answer that reports straight to a chief for
everything, and its opposite, the answer that protects the crew at any cost.
**Axis:** severity, the relationship to the offender, and whether the wrong is theft,
impairment, dishonesty, safety, or speech.
**Sources:** FH, TP.

1. You find out a firefighter on your crew has been taking supplies from the station. What do you do?
2. You're on a call and you see a crew member take something from a patient's home. What do you do?
3. A member of your crew arrives for shift and you're fairly sure he's been drinking. What do you do?
4. You're brand new. You see the senior firefighter on your crew do something you were taught in the academy is unsafe. What do you do?
5. A firefighter you like and respect asks you not to mention something you saw. What do you do?
6. You hear a crew member say something about a patient that you find offensive. What do you do?
7. You realise a report your officer filed contains something that isn't accurate. What do you do?
8. Tell us about a time in your own life when you saw someone do something wrong and had to decide whether to say anything.

## INT-3 — Refusing an order

**Listening for:** whether he can distinguish an order he dislikes from an order he should
not follow, and whether he understands when the objection is raised. The trap is both
extremes — blanket obedience, and a candidate who sounds like he will argue on scene.
**Axis:** danger vs. distaste, timing of the objection, and what he does afterward.
**Sources:** TP.

1. Would you ever disobey an order? Under what circumstances?
2. Your officer gives you an order on scene that you believe is unsafe. What do you do?
3. What's the difference between an order you disagree with and an order you shouldn't follow?
4. You're told to do something you think is wrong but not dangerous. What do you do?
5. Tell us about a time you followed an instruction you disagreed with. How did it turn out?
6. Tell us about a time you refused to do something you were asked to do at work.
7. You question an order on scene and are told to do it anyway. What then?
8. The call is over and you still think the order was wrong. What do you do about it?

## INT-4 — Harassment

**Listening for:** whether he will act when it is socially expensive, and whether he can
locate a line without either dismissing the issue or reciting policy language.
**Axis:** witness vs. participant vs. accused; the power of the person doing it; whether he
is alone in his view.
**Sources:** TP.

1. ~~What does harassment mean to you, and why does it matter in a firehouse?~~ **`STRIKE`** (Grant, 2026-09-10, pass 2; Red accepted) — definition / why-it-matters; written-test voice.
2. You witness a coworker being harassed by another member of the crew. What do you do?

5. Someone tells you that something you said offended them. What do you do?
6. What would you do if the person doing it was your officer?
7. What would you do if you were the only person on the crew who thought something had gone too far?
8. Tell us about a time you spoke up about how someone was being treated.

## INT-5 — Background

**Listening for:** whether he volunteers it plainly or minimises. The trap is discovering in
the background investigation what he chose not to say here.
**Axis:** who the disclosure concerns and how it would surface — employer, record, self-report.
**Sources:** TP.

1. We'll be running a full background investigation. Is there anything you'd like to explain to this panel now?
2. What will we find when we talk to your current or most recent employer?
3. Is there anything in your driving record we should know about?
4. What's the worst thing you've done that you'd have to tell us about?
5. Has anyone ever had reason not to trust you? What happened?
6. What would your last supervisor say you most need to work on?
7. Have you ever left a job on bad terms? Tell us about it.
8. Have you had to explain something in your past to an employer before? How did you explain it?

# STR — Composure under stress

*Criteria **c4 / c5**, Grant-approved / publishable 2026-09-10. Family pass 1 +
variant pass 2 complete 2026-09-10. Nothing publishes until Grant opens publish.*

> **Constraint reminder.** These items ask what he *did* and what he has *built*. Nothing
> here licenses scoring how his voice sounds. Voice-based confidence and emotion detection
> are permanently out (`CLAUDE.md`); the delivery layer is arithmetic on timestamps only.

## STR-1 — A real emergency or high-pressure event

**Listening for:** a specific incident with his own action in it. Per every source, a
candidate with no emergency background can still answer this well from ordinary life — an
item that assumes prior emergency experience is a defect.
**Axis:** his role, whether it went well, and whether he was the calm one or the one who froze.
**Sources:** FH, TP.

1. Tell us about a time you were present at an emergency. What did you do?
2. Tell us about a time you had to act before you felt ready.
3. Describe the most physically demanding situation you've been in.
4. Tell us about a time everything was going wrong at once. How did you sort it out?
5. Describe a time you were frightened and had to keep going anyway.
6. Tell us about a time you were the calmest person there.
7. Tell us about a time you froze, or did the wrong thing under pressure.
8. Describe a time you had to give instructions to people who were panicking.

## STR-2 — How he expects to handle the stresses

**Listening for:** whether he has thought concretely about the cumulative cost rather than
the dramatic moment, and whether he has anyone to talk to. The trap is the answer that
claims nothing bothers him.
**Axis:** the source of the stress — a call type, the schedule, the carry-home, someone else's
struggle.
**Sources:** SLC, TP.

1. What makes you think you can handle the stresses of this job?
2. This job will take you to calls involving children. How do you expect to handle that?
3. What do you think will be hardest for you to carry home?
4. How do you handle it when something is bothering you and you still have twelve hours left on shift?
5. What does stress look like on you? How would we know?
6. Who do you talk to when something has gone badly?
7. What do you do to recover after a hard week?
8. What would you do if you noticed a crewmate wasn't handling something well?

## STR-3 — What he has deliberately built

**Listening for:** preparation as a practice rather than a claim — something he does
repeatedly, with an account of what it changed. This is the composure analogue of MOT-3 and
scores the same way: motion and recency.

# JUD — Judgment & problem solving

*Criteria **c4 / c5**, Grant-approved / publishable 2026-09-10. Family pass 1 +
variant pass 2 complete 2026-09-10. Nothing publishes until Grant opens publish.*

## JUD-1 — A problem he solved

**Listening for:** the reasoning, not the outcome. Whether he can reconstruct how he decided
rather than only what happened.
**Axis:** resources available, whether the first attempt worked, and whether anyone asked him
to solve it.
**Sources:** CJ, TP.

1. Describe a problem you faced and how you solved it.
2. Tell us about a problem you solved with no one to ask for help.
3. Describe a problem where your first solution didn't work.
4. Tell us about a problem you spotted before anyone else did.
5. Describe a problem you solved that nobody asked you to solve.
6. Tell us about a problem you couldn't solve. What did you do?
7. Describe a time you had to fix something with only what you had on hand.
8. Tell us about a problem where the obvious answer was the wrong one.

## JUD-2 — A genuinely difficult decision

**Listening for:** what made it hard, and whether he owns the choice. The trap is a decision
that was not actually difficult, chosen because it flatters him.
**Axis:** time pressure, who it affected, what it cost, and whether he'd repeat it.
**Sources:** TP.

1. Describe a difficult decision you've had to make.
2. Tell us about a decision you had to make quickly with incomplete information.
3. Describe a decision that was going to disappoint someone no matter what you chose.
4. Tell us about a decision you made that you'd make differently now.
5. Describe a decision where doing the right thing cost you something.
6. Tell us about a decision you made on someone else's behalf.
7. Describe a decision you put off. What happened?
8. Tell us about a decision where you went against advice you'd been given.

## JUD-3 — Ranking personal priorities — **TRAP FAMILY**

**Listening for:** the reasoning, explicitly. There is no correct ranking and the panel is
not scoring the order — candidates routinely answer as though there is a right answer and
try to find it.
**Axis:** what is being traded off — life domains, safety, loyalties, virtues.
**Sources:** TP.

> **Pillar 3 material.** The trap here is the single most nameable one in the whole bank:
> *the question is scored on the defence, not the order.* Worth teaching directly.

1. Rank these in order of importance to you: career, family, friends. Explain your reasoning.
2. Rank these: your own safety, your crew's safety, the public's safety. Explain your reasoning.
3. Your family needs you and you're on shift. What do you do?
4. What matters more — being good at this job, or being trusted by the people you work with?
5. Rank these for a probationary firefighter: learning fast, fitting in, speaking up. Explain.
6. Where does this job sit against everything else in your life, honestly?
7. What would you give up for this career, and what wouldn't you?
8. Rank these: honesty, loyalty, obedience. Explain your reasoning.

## JUD-4 — Ranking parts of the mission — **TRAP FAMILY**
