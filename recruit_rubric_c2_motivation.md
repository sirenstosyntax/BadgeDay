## Scoring notes

These govern how the anchors below are applied. They are part of the criterion, not commentary on it.

**1. Effort is scored relative to opportunity.**
Weigh evidence of pursuit against the time, money, and access the candidate has had — not against an absolute checklist. A candidate one year out of high school holding two certifications and a volunteer slot outscores a candidate with five years, a part-time job, and the same two certifications. Qualification count alone is not the measure.

*Consequence:* a 4 may hold fewer qualifications on paper than a 3. This is intended. The criterion measures trajectory, not inventory.

**2. This criterion is partly subjective, and that is not a defect.**
Panels react to candidates; they do not only measure them. A rubric clean enough to be fully objective would describe something that does not happen in the room. The anchors below are behavioral so they can be verified. The *reaction* to those behaviors is real and is named where it bears on the score.

**3. Critique voice constraint.**
Generated critique may state how an answer is **likely to read to a panel**, tied to a specific observable behavior. It may not assert a score the candidate received, or attribute an internal state.

- Permitted: "Six certifications listed in forty seconds with no account of why any mattered — that reads as résumé recital."
- Rejected: "You came across as arrogant." / "You don't seem to genuinely care."

**4. Anchors describe motion, never eligibility.**
Minimum qualifications vary by department and change over time — some departments hire without EMT-B on a probationary clock, others screen for it before the panel convenes. No anchor below references whether the candidate meets a minimum, because that phrase means something different in every jurisdiction. What is scored is direction and rate: what he has done, when he last did it, and what he says comes next.

**5. The criterion is compensatory; the score alone is not diagnostic.**
Two axes are in play: accumulated evidence of pursuit, and depth of drive. A 5 is high on both. A 4 is high on one. Because two candidates reach the same number by opposite routes, the score must carry a route tag (4A / 4B), and generated critique must address the axis that is short. Feedback keyed to the number rather than the route will tell a candidate to do more of what he is already doing.

Depth of drive is read from what the effort was aimed at: qualifying for the job, or becoming good at it. The second shows up as pursuit nobody required, an account of what it changed, and a motive that points at the people served rather than at the candidate.

**6. Red flags are not contained.**
Panel behavior is not answer-independent. A flag raised early is reported to shape interpretation of subsequent answers rather than being scored in isolation. A pipeline that scores each answer independently and sums will not reproduce real board outcomes.

## Anchors

### 5 — Continuous, self-directed pursuit; motive is other-focused and evidenced

Describes an unbroken line from the decision point to today, including steps nobody required of him. Can say what a qualification **changed about how he thinks**, not merely that he holds it. Names people, places, and specifics without prompting. When he describes the work, the subject of the sentence is usually someone other than himself. Service motive is demonstrated through an instance, not asserted.

### 4 — Strong on one axis, short on the other

Two candidates reach a 4 by opposite routes. The score is the same; the diagnosis is not. **Record which.**

**4A — Drive high, evidence thin.**
Working as hard as circumstances allow and chasing every exposure available to him — ride-alongs, station visits, whatever he can get to — with a shorter file than a 5. Enrolled, testing, next step named with a date on it. Can say what he has learned and what he is after. The gap is runway, not effort.

**4B — Evidence strong, drive shallower.**
Holds the qualifications and went past what was required rather than stopping at it — can point to something he pursued that nobody asked for, and say what it changed. But the pursuit reads as aimed at *qualifying* rather than at becoming good at the work. Competent, prepared, and not relentless.

Boundary against 3: any real step past the eligibility list, with something to show for it, is a 4B. Stopping exactly at the list is a 3.

### 3 — Qualified, willing, and stopped there

Holds what the posting required and completed it in good faith; the willingness to do the work is real. But preparation maps exactly to the eligibility list with nothing past it. Can state qualifications; cannot say what any of them changed. Reasons for wanting the job are correct and general — public service, helping people, every day is different — with no incident behind them. Meeting the requirement reads, to him, as being finished.

### 2 — Preparation is real but stale

Took steps toward the job at some point and has done nothing since. Classes are years old. Nothing has been added, renewed, or built on. When asked what he has been doing lately, the answer is about circumstances rather than actions. He can describe what he did; he cannot put a recent date on any of it, and cannot say what comes next.

Distinguish from a 4: both files are incomplete. The 4's gap is explained by a short runway and he is visibly closing it. The 2 has had the time and has not used it. Same distance from the finish, opposite direction of travel.

### 1 — Unprepared, with no account of why

Nothing done since deciding, and no account of why. Cannot name a class, a ride-along, a station visit, a conversation, or a date. Interest in the job is stated and has never been acted on.

## Cross-references

**Prepared but self-focused → Criterion 3 (Teamwork & Interpersonal).**
The candidate with a strong file whose every answer returns to himself is **not** penalized here. He may legitimately score 4 or 5 on Motivation & Preparation. The failure is one of fit, and it is scored where it occurs.

> **Open risk:** until Teamwork carries an explicit anchor for the *qualified* self-focused candidate, this candidate passes through the rubric unpenalized. Author that anchor first when Criterion 3 is written.

## Provenance and limitations

**These anchors represent one captain's scoring judgment.** They are not a national standard and not a survey of practice. Departments vary in structure, weighting, eligibility rules, and culture. A candidate scored against this rubric is being told how *this* panelist would read the answer — which is useful, and is not the same as a prediction of outcome. Product copy should not overstate it.

**Division of labor between sources.** Published material was used for coverage — dimension names, scoring conventions, board structure. It was not used for anchors. Generic sources describe the top performer in interchangeable adjectives and do not distinguish adjacent scores, which is the only part of a rubric that produces actionable critique.

**Source incentive warning.** Most publicly available oral board material is authored by vendors selling preparation services, who have a commercial interest in presenting the board as decodable. Apply the vendor-authorship screening rule to any source proposed for filling an anchor gap, not merely for background.

**Path to generalizability.** The remedy for single-panelist bias is additional panelists, not additional web content. Target two or three captains from departments with differing board formats, scoring against these anchors independently. Agreement indicates trade-wide judgment. Disagreement should be documented as a split rather than averaged away — a candidate is better served knowing a dimension is contested than being given a false consensus.

## Sources consulted

External material was used to check scoring convention only. No anchor text is derived from it.

- Lepore (battalion chief, entry and promotional panels) — panels resolve to three questions: academy, probation, and whether the crew will respect the candidate. Supports scoring self-focus under Teamwork rather than Motivation.
- Smith, *Top Scoring Oral Interview Strategies* — the "clone answer" pattern. Independent corroboration of the level 3 anchor.
- Fire Service Selection — red-flag halo effect across subsequent answers. Basis for scoring note 4.
- **Dimension frame — the convergent core, not any one instrument.** The dimensions this rubric sits within (teamwork, interpersonal skill, composure, judgment, communication, adaptability, integrity, motivation and preparation, service orientation) are taken from what recurs across *independently authored* public sources — principally Unified Fire Authority's published testing-process dimensions and Salt Lake City Fire's practice oral board material, both department-authored. A dimension earns a place in the frame by appearing in more than one such source. See `recruit_oral_board_sources.md` for the convergence table.

  **On I/O Solutions.** Their entry-level oral assessment guide, published under City of Tulsa letterhead, was read during the source pass and is **excluded as a frame source** — it is vendor material, and `CLAUDE.md` prohibits deriving Recruit content from commercial hiring-test batteries and equivalents. A vendor's particular set and phrasing of dimensions is its product, and a `.gov` URL does not change that. It contributed no dimension to the frame above that is not independently corroborated by a department-authored source. One general observation from it — that delivery is conventionally rated separately from answer content — is a scoring convention widely enough attested to stand on its own, and is reflected here as a separate delivery sub-score rather than as anything taken from that document.
