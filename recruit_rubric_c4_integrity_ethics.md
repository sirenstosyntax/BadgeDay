## Why this criterion uses a designated question plus corroboration

Panels almost always put a deliberate integrity probe on the board — a mistake and what
changed, a peer doing something wrong, whether he would refuse an order, harassment, or
something in his background he wants to explain. That probe is where candidates walk into
traps: covering for a buddy, never refusing any order, or a polished “I have never made a
mistake.”

Scoring integrity **only** on that probe is how a candidate escapes when he nails the
rehearsed ethics speech and still shifts blame, minimizes harm, or refuses to own a miss
everywhere else.

Scoring integrity **only** across the whole board with no designated probe understates what
panels actually do: they ask the hard question on purpose.

**Settled 2026-09-10:** treat this like Criterion 2 — **designated integrity question is
primary evidence; corroborating tells from the rest of the board are admissible.** The
designated answer can carry the band alone when it is clear. Corroboration raises or lowers
when the rest of the board shows the same pattern or the opposite one. **Corroboration may
move the designated band by at most one** (Grant-approved 2026-09-10, captain pass).

## What this criterion is for

A panel is deciding whether they can trust this person with other people’s lives, other
people’s property, and the department’s name. Politeness is not integrity. Loyalty that
covers a wrong is not integrity. A correct speech about honesty is not integrity.

What is scored is **conduct and ownership**: will he tell the truth when it costs him, act
when a peer is wrong, refuse an order that would put someone in danger or break the law, and
own a mistake with a real change afterward — not a slogan.

Self-awareness feeds this criterion when it shows up as owning a miss; most self-awareness
weight still belongs on Criterion 5 (Judgment & Composure), per Grant’s naming note.

## Scoring notes

These govern how the anchors below are applied. They are part of the criterion.

**1. Ownership outranks polish.**

An awkward, specific ownership of a real miss outranks a smooth speech about always being
honest. The first job is whether he will put the truth on the table when it costs him.

**2. Protecting the public and the crew outranks protecting a buddy or himself.**

Where a peer is stealing, lying, impaired, or cutting a corner that can hurt someone, the
question is whether he acts — not whether he stays popular. Going to the person first when
that is safe and appropriate is good. **It is not enough by itself** when harm, theft,
impairment, or illegal conduct is possible: entry-level must escalate past private peer talk
onto an officer/supervisor path. Stopping at “I talked to him” on those stakes is a miss.
**Do not score “I would never get a teammate in trouble” as loyalty.** On a crew that is how
people get hurt.


**3. Refusing an order is not the same as insubordination.**

A candidate who says he would never refuse any order has not shown integrity — he has shown
he has not thought about an unlawful or immediately unsafe order. A candidate who refuses
because he is inconvenienced or embarrassed has shown the wrong refusal. Score whether he
can name **when** refusal is required (safety, legality, clear harm) versus when it is not.

**4. Specificity is a credibility floor.**

An answer that names what happened, what he did, and what changed can be believed. “I always
do the right thing” cannot be checked. Score whether there is purchase on something real —
not whether the story is well told.

**5. Critique voice constraint.**

Generated critique may state how an answer is **likely to read to a panel**, tied to a
specific observable behavior in what he said. It may not assert a score the candidate
received, or attribute an internal state.

- Permitted: "He describes covering a short count so nobody would look bad — that reads as
  protecting the crew’s image over the truth."
- Rejected: "You are dishonest." / "You don't have a moral compass." / "The panel won't trust
  you."

**6. Three outcomes, not two.**

Rubric-wide rule, settled on Criterion 3 and restated on Criteria 1–2. Stated here for this
criterion.

**Where he gave nothing to judge either way, return *not answered*.** Silence, a refusal, a
pivot that never returns. Not a low score and not a finding about him.

**Where the material positively establishes that integrity cannot be assessed from what he
gave, return *insufficient evidence to assess*.** Its own outcome, never averaged in as a
zero, and it must quote what establishes it. Example shape: he has never seen peer theft or
impairment **and** offers no smaller integrity-relevant occasion when invited — not a 1 for
a clean record. **Getting this backwards tells a man he has no integrity, on evidence nobody
has.**

**It is not an escape hatch for anchor 1.** A thin “I am honest” speech *has* integrity
content — it is weak, and weak is what the low bands score. Insufficient evidence requires
that there is nothing scorable, not that what is there is poor.

**Default when in doubt is *not answered*,** and reaching for insufficient evidence needs
the answer to establish that there is nothing to score. Integrity has a smaller “genuinely
empty life” population than Teamwork’s solo workers, but a candidate who has never faced a
peer-misconduct scene is real — follow the lead to a smaller occasion before scoring empty.

**7. Do not score the crime; score what he did about it.**

A candidate who worked near someone dishonest is not penalized for their presence. A
candidate with an easy history is not credited for luck. Score his action and his ownership.
A candidate who describes a real problem honestly is doing better than one who reports that
nothing wrong has ever happened around him — the second answer is often not true, and is
sometimes the bigger flag.

**8. Red flags are not contained.**

An integrity flag — covering, lying, following an order that would clearly hurt someone — is
reported to shape how the rest of the board is read. Panel behavior is not answer-independent,
and a trust concern colours everything afterward.

**9. This criterion is partly subjective, and that is not a defect.**

Panels react to whether they trust him. The anchors stay behavioral so they can be verified;
the reaction to those behaviors is named where it bears on the score. Subjectivity is not
permission to invent motive or character labels.

**10. Background-to-explain is scored on honesty and accountability, not on the existence of
a past.**

Having something to explain is not an automatic low band. Minimizing, blaming, or refusing
to name what he owns is. A clean file with no occasion to discuss is not a 5 by itself —
the designated probe still has to show how he handles an integrity demand.

**11. Corroboration may move the designated band by at most one.**

The designated integrity question is primary. Corroborating tells from the rest of the board
may raise or lower that band by **at most one**. They cannot jump two bands. The designated
answer can carry the band alone when it is clear.

## Anchors

### 5 — Owns the costly truth; acts when it matters; can say what changed

On the designated probe he puts a real occasion on the table: a mistake and what changed, a
peer’s wrongdoing and what he did, a line he would not cross on an order, harassment he
recognized and addressed, or a background fact he owns without spinning it into someone
else’s fault. Claims are specific enough to check. Where someone could get hurt or the truth
was on the line, he acted toward the truth rather than toward comfort — and on peer harm,
theft, impairment, or illegal conduct, that includes escalating past private talk to an
officer/supervisor path, not stopping at a quiet word. He can say **what changed afterward**
— not only that he “learned a lesson.”

Corroboration across the board matches: he owns misses elsewhere rather than distributing
blame, and he does not treat loyalty as cover.

### 4 — Right direction, short of the top band

The designated answer shows the right instinct — he would not cover, he would not follow an
unlawful or immediately unsafe order, he owns a miss — but something is thin: the occasion
is light, the change afterward is vague, or he needed the question framed before he would
go there. Still mostly specific. Corroboration does not contradict him.

**Boundary against 3.** A 4 requires at least one checkable occasion or a clear, concrete
rule for when he would refuse or escalate — not only “I value integrity.”

**Boundary against 5.** A 5 shows costly ownership and a real change, as a pattern the rest
of the board does not undercut. A 4 has the direction and is short on cost, change, or
consistency.

### 3 — Correct generalities; little to check

Says the right things in the abstract — honesty matters, he would not steal, he would report
something serious — without a checkable occasion, or with an occasion so thin it could belong
to anyone. Does not clearly choose cover over truth, but also does not show he has paid for
the truth. A panel hears a speech, not a person they can verify.

### 2 — Protects comfort, image, or a buddy over the truth

Pattern on the designated answer and/or corroboration:

- Would cover, stay quiet, or stop at private peer talk on theft, dishonesty, impairment,
  harm, or illegal conduct — with no officer/supervisor path — to “not get anyone in
  trouble.”
- Minimizes his own mistake; nothing meaningful changed afterward.
- Treats any refusal of an order as disloyalty, with no safety or legality line.
- Recognizes harassment (or similar) and still chooses silence for belonging.
- Background explanation shifts blame and never lands on what he owns.

Distinguish from 3: the 3 is empty and correct-sounding. The 2 tells you something, and the
something is a trust problem.

### 1 — Would cover, lie, or go along when it can hurt someone

States he would hide the truth, falsify, cover impairment or theft, follow an order he
himself describes as unlawful or immediately dangerous, or treats the integrity probe with
contempt. Or the board shows open dishonesty about his own record. This is a trust failure
a panel does not have to infer — he said it.

## Cross-references

**Answer Construction → Criterion 1.**
A well-built answer that covers a wrong is still a low band here. Construction does not
rescue integrity.

**Motivation & Preparation → Criterion 2.**
A strong file does not buy a pass on trust. He may be a 5 on Motivation and a 2 here —
coherent, not a contradiction.

**Teamwork & Interpersonal → Criterion 3.**
Criterion 3’s “will he say the difficult thing” overlaps. Peer-misconduct items are scored
**here** when the stake is truth, safety, or policy — not only interpersonal comfort.
Self-focus without a cover-up stays on Criterion 3.

**Judgment & Composure → Criterion 5.**
Hard calls under stress, ranking traps, and most self-awareness weight land on C5. This
criterion takes the ethics/ownership core; do not double-penalize the same sentence on both
without saying which part each score is reading.
